Worrydream deep-dive is done. Full notes in `vault/Research/BretVictor-Worrydream-Takeaways.md`. Here's the synthesis:

---

**The central argument** (across all essays): Software design is misdiagnosed as an *interaction* problem when it's actually a *representation* problem. Most software is "information software" — it should be designed as context-sensitive graphic design. Interactivity is a last resort, not a feature.

That's a genuinely radical reframe. The entire HCI field is built on "how do users interact with systems." Victor says the question is wrong. *What does the system show, and does it show the right thing without being asked?*

---

**By essay:**

**Magic Ink (2006)** — the foundation. Argues that information software should anticipate what users want to know based on context (environment, history, inferred intent) and just *show it*. A train schedule app shouldn't ask "what time?" — it should know you commute at 8am and show that. The redesign of Amazon and Yahoo Movies he includes are genuinely better than what actually shipped in 2006 and still better than most UIs today.

**Learnable Programming (2012)** — a full design spec for programming environments. The four requirements: show the data at all times, make time tangible (scrub through execution), eliminate hidden state, let programmers create by reacting first then abstracting. This is the essay that directly inspired Khan Academy CS and kicked off the whole "live coding" movement — but he explicitly says Khan Academy missed the point. Live coding is not the same as *seeing your program*.

**Climate Change (2015)** — the most ambitious and strangest. He argues that programming language quality is a climate issue. Scientific computing runs on R and MATLAB — 40-year-old cruft — which means climate models are harder to write, slower to validate, and less accessible than they should be. His concrete claim: **contributing to Julia is a climate action**. Also: the smart grid is fundamentally a distributed algorithms problem that nobody in energy is framing that way. And the most striking data point in the whole site: **all batteries on Earth combined store less than 10 minutes of world energy consumption**.

---

**Most surprising claims across all of it:**
- "Pictures Under Glass" (touchscreens) is a transitional technology, not the destination — we've designed interfaces for a world where hands can only poke flat surfaces
- The power grid needs something like TCP/IP — an open protocol layer for distributed energy that doesn't exist yet
- Programming is currently designed to be understood by machines, not humans — "to understand a program you must become both the machine and the program" is Alan Perlis's view, which Victor says is the core mistake keeping programming hard
- Seeing Spaces (the fabrication lab concept) proposes that every workshop should have sensor arrays and cameras so you can *see* inside what you're building in real time — X-ray vision as standard tooling

---

**What the site itself does right:** the essays are interactive demonstrations of their own arguments. *Magic Ink* shows redesigned interfaces inline. *Learnable Programming* has live code examples where you can see exactly the problems he's describing. The site doesn't have a nav bar, doesn't push you anywhere, doesn't ask for your email. It just exists and shows you things. That's the thesis made manifest.

**Essential reading order:** Magic Ink → Learnable Programming → Climate Change → Up and Down the Ladder of Abstraction → Inventing on Principle (video, watch it).
