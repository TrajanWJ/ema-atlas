# Works in Progress — Key Takeaways
*25 posts read | 2026-03-20*

## The Big Ideas

1. **Ideas and institutions drive growth more than material factors.** Joel Mokyr's Nobel-worthy thesis (and WiP's founding principle): the Industrial Revolution happened in Britain because useful knowledge was accumulated, organized, and shared through scientific societies, publications, and the combination of "propositional knowledge" (what scientists know) with "prescriptive knowledge" (what craftsmen can do). Coal and colonies were secondary.

2. **The West is systematically failing to build.** Whether cities, housing, transit, or innovative companies, the post-WWII regulatory settlement has created systems optimized for protecting existing stakeholders at the expense of creating new value. The Great Downzoning, European labor laws, and NIMBYism are all manifestations of the same underlying problem.

3. **AI is not replacing expert knowledge workers — yet.** The radiology case study is a paradigmatic example: AI outperforms humans on benchmarks but demand for human radiologists is at all-time highs. The reasons (behavior change in clinical settings, malpractice liability, task diversity, Jevons paradox) are structural, not just technical lags. But this isn't a permanent stay of execution.

4. **Low fertility is a civilizational crisis with clear structural causes.** South Korea is the canary: career-motherhood conflict, hyper-intensive parenting culture (80% of children in private cram schools), gender polarization, and antinatalist policy legacy all compound. Pro-child subsidies do work — but need to be dramatically larger to compete with these forces.

5. **Progress requires accepting failure costs.** European companies can't build Teslas because firing workers costs 31–62 months of salary per head (vs. 7 in the US). This makes innovation — which requires iterative failure — unaffordable. The solution isn't abandoning worker protection but redesigning it (Danish flexicurity) to protect workers' *incomes* rather than their *specific jobs*.

## Standout Claims

1. **Joel Mokyr won the Nobel for work most economists would never publish** ("What Makes Joel Mokyr Great") — his qualitative, narrative, books-based economic history would be rejected by most economics journals today, which require large datasets and statistical methods. His prize is a "signal from the economics profession that it doesn't just value fancy statistical methods." Britain's Industrial Revolution happened because its scientific and literary societies uniquely combined propositional and prescriptive knowledge.

2. **AI is not replacing radiologists — demand is at all-time highs** ("AI Isn't Replacing Radiologists") — radiology residency positions hit a record 1,208 in 2025, a 4% increase from 2024. Average salary: $520,000, up 48% from 2015. Despite 700+ FDA-cleared AI radiology models, radiologists spend only 36% of their time on direct image interpretation. The rest is patient communication, overseeing scans, teaching, and clinical coordination. Jevons paradox: as scans became faster/cheaper, doctors ordered far more of them.

3. **Classical statues were probably NOT painted the way modern reconstructions suggest** ("Classical Statues Were Not Painted Horribly") — Vinzenz Brinkmann's famous colored reconstructions look garish and ugly, but ancient depictions of statues in Roman mosaics and frescoes show them as delicately and naturalistically painted, nothing like the modern reconstructions. The most likely explanation: the reconstructions are painted badly, not that ancient Romans had alien color taste. The reconstructors may even be "trolling" the public.

4. **South Korea's fertility disaster was partly caused by its own government** ("Why Is South Korean Fertility So Low?") — a 1960s–80s antinatalist campaign cut fertility from 6 to 2 children per woman in 25 years (vs. 96 years in the UK). The resulting population age structure and cultural normalization of childlessness created negative momentum that pro-child subsidies can slow but not reverse. Today only 28% of unmarried South Koreans aged 19–49 want children.

5. **European companies can't fire workers at all** ("Why Europe Doesn't Have a Tesla") — restructuring in Germany costs 31 months of salary per employee, France 38, Italy 52, Spain 62 — vs. 7 months in the US. VW has guaranteed German factory jobs since 1994. When the company's CEO tried to warn about falling behind Tesla, the Porsche/Piech family owners fired *him*. VW ultimately had to license core EV software from American startup Rivian.

6. **The Great Downzoning was driven by property owners, not planning ideology** ("Why the West Was Downzoned") — in the 1890s, German suburbs introduced differential area zoning to protect property values. It spread globally because it protected existing homeowners, not because planners wanted low density. Where downzoning *reduced* land values (greenfield development), landowners successfully blocked it. When planners *changed their minds* about density in the 1990s, suburbs remained zoned the same anyway — because homeowner interests hadn't changed.

7. **Victorian cities grew 20–5,000x in a century while house prices stayed flat** ("Many Victorian Cities Grew by Tenfold in a Century") — London's population grew ~8x, Sydney's ~240x, Chicago's ~5,000x between 1800 and 1914. Real house prices remained flat the entire time despite enormous demand increases, because the right to build was largely unrestricted. In Britain, average house price went from 12x average earnings in 1850 to 4x in 1914. Today it's back to 9x.

8. **Nineteenth-century cities had better public transit than we do today** ("Many Victorian Cities Grew") — the US had 975 electric tram networks with 45,000 miles of track in 1914. Average speed of a New York tram in 1914: 8 mph. Average speed of New York buses today: 7.8 mph. The Victorian system was funded through regulated monopolies that charged profitable user fees, not public subsidies — which is why it proliferated, and why it collapsed when post-WWI inflation eroded the real value of fare controls.

9. **Korean students have 6am–2am school days** ("Why Is South Korean Fertility So Low?") — 80% of children attend private cram schools (*hagwons*). Boarding *hagwons* run 6am to midnight. The neighborhood of Daechi in Seoul has "screaming pods" for teenagers to vent frustration. Parents in the bottom income quintile spend as much on tutoring as on food. The government banned private tutoring in the 1980s; tutors simply went underground and charged "risk premiums."

10. **The Olley-Pakes measure may be the "holy grail" of competition policy** ("The Holy Grail of Capitalism") — standard measures (market concentration, markups) often give the wrong signal: rising concentration can mean a market is *working* if efficient companies are gaining share. Olley-Pakes measures whether productive companies are actually gaining market share over less productive ones. It correctly identified that post-2008 UK stagnation was caused by zombie companies tying up workers, not by traditional monopoly problems — something concentration and markup measures missed entirely.

11. **Antinatalism that "worked" can create cultures that don't want children** ("South Korean Fertility") — France went from Europe's lowest fertility in the 1920s to highest today after an active pro-natal campaign. South Tyrol in Italy has the country's highest fertility after generous childcare subsidies and monthly payments. Cash subsidies *do* raise birth rates in South Korea — a 10% increase in bonus raises fertility 0.34–0.58%. But they can't overcome decades of cultural normalization of childlessness.

12. **Nokia built a touchscreen internet phone prototype around 2000 and still lost** ("Why Europe Doesn't Have a Tesla") — Nokia was the world's largest smartphone maker and had prototype touchscreen internet devices years before the iPhone. It didn't develop the software because it couldn't afford to bear the innovation risk: when Nokia closed one German phone plant in 2008, it cost €200 million to fire just 2,000 employees.

13. **South Korea may face a dependency ratio so severe that young people start emigrating** ("South Korean Fertility") — for Korea to maintain even Japan's dependency ratio (the worst in the OECD), it would need a fertility rate of 4.2 children per woman. At some point the remaining young may leave for less tax-burdened countries, worsening the spiral. It files more patents in absolute terms than any country except the US (with 1/6th the population). This innovation capacity could be lost.

14. **Denmark's flexicurity model shows a different path** ("Why Europe Doesn't Have a Tesla") — Danish employers can fire almost at will (like the US), but workers receive unemployment insurance covering up to 90% of previous income for 2 years, plus aggressive government retraining support (2% of GDP). Result: Denmark has Novo Nordisk and Roche-level innovation. The Danes and Swiss are "Europe's innovation heavyweights." The insight: protect workers' *incomes*, not their *specific jobs*.

## Best Posts (ranked)

1. **"Why Europe Doesn't Have a Tesla"** — The firing-cost analysis is the clearest explanation I've seen for why Europe consistently fails at radical innovation. The VW case study (board blocking factory closures, CEO fired for warning about Tesla) is damning. The Nokia story is the best supporting anecdote.

2. **"Why the West Was Downzoned"** — Extraordinary long-form piece showing the Great Downzoning was driven by homeowner self-interest, not planning ideology. The evidence for this (downzoning succeeded where it raised property values, failed where it lowered them; planners changed their minds in 1990s but suburbs didn't change) is compelling. High relevance for housing policy.

3. **"Many Victorian Cities Grew by Tenfold in a Century"** — Remarkable argument that 19th century cities were actually better governed in key ways: self-funding infrastructure monopolies, right to build, flat house prices despite massive demand. The transit speed comparison (1914 tram vs. 2025 NYC bus: 8 mph vs. 7.8 mph) is a devastating single statistic.

4. **"AI Isn't Replacing Radiologists"** — Best empirical case study on AI-and-labor displacement. The finding that clinical AI performance drops up to 20 points from benchmark performance due to training data limitations and behavior change is crucial. The malpractice exclusion clauses ("Absolute AI Exclusion") are a real regulatory story nobody else is covering.

5. **"Why Is South Korean Fertility So Low?"** — Comprehensive and genuinely alarming. The hagwon schedule details are jaw-dropping. The MeToo backlash → political gender polarization → fertility collapse causal chain is well-argued. The comparison with France's pro-natal success shows policy can work.

6. **"What Makes Joel Mokyr Great"** — Short and excellent. Makes the case that qualitative economic history is legitimate Nobel-worthy work in an era of p-value obsession. The propositional/prescriptive knowledge framework for understanding the Industrial Revolution is actionable for thinking about AI.

7. **"Classical Statues Were Not Painted Horribly"** — Starts as a niche art history puzzle and becomes a meditation on expert credibility and public trust. The "trolling" hypothesis for why ugly reconstructions persist is unexpectedly plausible.

8. **"The Holy Grail of Capitalism"** — Dense but important. The Olley-Pakes framework could genuinely improve antitrust policy. The Colombia example (liberalization → productivity growth through market reallocation, not within-firm improvement) is counterintuitive.

## Recurring Arguments

**Core thesis:** Progress is not automatic or inevitable — it requires specific institutional conditions: the right to experiment and fail, mechanisms that reward productive firms over unproductive ones, combination of theoretical knowledge with practical skill, and investment in public goods like streets, drains, and transit that have spillover effects. Western societies have progressively undermined these conditions through regulatory capture by incumbent interests (homeowners, workers in declining industries, established firms).

**Recurring subthemes:**
- Ideas and knowledge organization are the primary cause of economic growth, not resources or geography
- The right to build (housing, companies, infrastructure) is being systematically curtailed, at enormous cost
- AI's deployment is slower and more complex than benchmarks suggest, due to behavior change, regulatory hurdles, task diversity, and Jevons paradox
- Population decline is the sleeper civilizational risk; pronatalist policies work but are too small
- Competition policy needs better measurement tools; current metrics often point in the wrong direction
- 19th-century institutions (regulated monopolies, right to build, user-funded infrastructure) solved collective action problems more elegantly than modern subsidy-dependent systems
- Progress in biology/medicine is real and accelerating (vaccines, RNA therapy, genome sequencing) even as physical world progress stagnates

**What distinguishes WiP:** Unlike most "progress" publications, it engages seriously with failure and complexity — why AI *doesn't* replace radiologists, why pro-natal policies in Korea *aren't* working enough, why good planning ideas don't get implemented. It's empirically careful and historically grounded. The house style is long-form, heavily sourced, comfortable with nuance. The founding principle explicitly traces to Mokyr's thesis that publishing and sharing useful knowledge is what makes progress happen.
